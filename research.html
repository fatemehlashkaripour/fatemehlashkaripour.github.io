Research

My research asks how labor market shocks and policies change health and risky behavior, and who bears the cost, especially the harm that falls on people who were never directly affected.

Job Market Paper

Import Competition, Impaired Driving, and Harm to Others JMP

Earlier title: Driving through Decline: Trade Exposure and Alcohol-Involved Fatal Crashes

PDF

Policies and shocks that eliminate jobs are usually judged by their effects on the workers who are displaced. This paper measures a cost that has been left out: risky behavior that harms people who did not choose the risk and are not compensated for it. I study the rise of Chinese import competition, a shock concentrated in manufacturing regions whose jobs did not return, and focus on alcohol-impaired driving, which separates the risk drivers take on themselves from the risk they impose on others. Using records of every fatal crash on U.S. public roads from 1991 to 2019, covering more than a million working-age drivers, I measure each commuting zone’s cumulative exposure to Chinese imports and instrument it with the same industries’ imports from China into eight other high-income countries. More exposed commuting zones had about 13 percent more working-age drivers at or above the legal alcohol limit in fatal crashes than similar zones in the same state and year (16 percent within the same Census division and year), roughly 630 more drivers a year nationally. The increase appears at every alcohol threshold and is not explained by changes in testing. Deaths of third parties rise by about 9 percent, or about 430 deaths a year, at a cost of roughly $4.7 billion a year. Pedestrian deaths do not rise, pointing to collisions between vehicles as the main channel. Self-reported survey data do not show this response, a reminder of the limits of surveys for measuring risky behavior. Because this harm is unpriced by firms and underpriced by drivers, it belongs in the evaluation of any policy that causes job displacement.

Working Papers

Both papers follow the same thread as my job market paper: how substance use and risky behavior change when policies and shocks change.

The Evolution of Cannabis Consumption: Evidence from Traffic Fatalities

with Amani Rashid and Caroline Weber

Presented at the Southern Economic Association Annual Meeting, Washington, DC, 2024

We use traffic fatalities to trace how cannabis consumption has changed over time.

Storms of Change: Exploring the Impact of Hurricanes on Substance Use

A hurricane, like a permanent job loss, is a shock people do not choose. This paper studies how substance use changes in the places hurricanes hit, asking how people adjust risky behavior after a shock in a new setting.

Publications

Reduction in the Introduction of Complementary Foods Prior to 4 Months and Prior to 6 Months: An Examination of the U.S. National Survey of Children’s Health 2016–2023

(Breastfeeding Medicine, 2025 – with Angela G. Campbell)

Using the National Survey of Children’s Health, 2016–2023, we study when infants are first given complementary foods (foods other than breast milk or formula) and show that fewer infants received them before 4 months and before 6 months over this period.

Research Agenda

  • Following the harm beyond the crash site. My current estimates count crashes where they happen. With restricted mortality files, I will measure how many residents of exposed places died and how many victims lived elsewhere; with hospital and emergency department records, I will capture nonfatal crash injuries.
  • Who pays. When a bystander is injured, the bill is split among insurers, the victim, and hospitals that absorb unpaid care. Using state hospital records and medical debt data, I will trace how job loss in one place shifts medical costs onto insurers, taxpayers, and local hospitals, and test whether unemployment insurance, Trade Adjustment Assistance, and Medicaid expansion offset this harm.
  • Beyond the China shock. The same design applies to other shocks that erased jobs in particular industries and places: NAFTA, industrial robots, the coal collapse, and the AI boom, which is now reaching occupations that have never faced this kind of shock.
  • Health care markets. Prices in health care are set by bargaining, and consolidation has shifted who pays and who profits. I plan to study how insurer pricing and hospital finances respond to mergers, vertical integration, and private equity entry, and what these changes mean for patients’ premiums and access, physicians’ pay and autonomy, and shareholders.
