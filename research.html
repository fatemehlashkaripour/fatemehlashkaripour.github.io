Job Market Paper

The China Shock and Death on American Roads

PDF Abstract

I estimate how the persistent labor demand shock caused by rising Chinese import competition affected alcohol-impaired driving on U.S. roads and the deaths of other road users. Using the federal record of every fatal crash from 1991 to 2019, I find that commuting zones more exposed to import competition had about 13 percent more working-age impaired drivers involved in fatal crashes than less exposed zones in the same state. I find increases for both male and female drivers, with men accounting for approximately 75 percent of the increase. While the China shock is commonly associated with China’s entry into the WTO in 2001, Chinese import competition had already been increasing throughout the 1990s. Its effects on U.S. manufacturing slowly began in the 1990s, intensified after 2001, and persisted for years as manufacturing plants closed and workers lost their jobs. In my preferred outcomes, I do not find an increase in the 1990s. The effect begins to emerge gradually after 2001, with the largest increase occurring after 2007, and persists through 2019. These findings suggest that the effects of persistent local labor demand shocks on traffic fatalities may differ from those of short-term economic downturns. I also find a 14 percent increase in the deaths of other people in crashes in which a working-age driver was impaired or has a recorded driving error, equivalent to approximately 870 additional deaths annually when scaled to the national population. Using the value of a statistical life, I estimate the annual value of these deaths at approximately $9.5 billion. These findings point to a potential external cost of trade-induced labor market disruption that could reduce the net gains from trade.
