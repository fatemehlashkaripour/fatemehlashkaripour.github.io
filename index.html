About me

I am a PhD candidate in Public Policy at the Martin School of Public Policy and Administration, University of Kentucky.

I work at the intersection of health policy and applied microeconomics. My research asks how economic shocks and different policies change health and risky behavior, and who ends up bearing the cost. Health responds to almost every economic and policy change, including many never meant to affect it, yet evaluations usually count only the people directly targeted. I focus on the harm that spills over onto everyone else.

I use quasi-experimental methods to study how communities respond to economic shocks and policies in the short and long run, how those responses shape local equilibrium, and what they imply for welfare.

I expect to receive my Ph.D. in 2027. I hold an M.Sc. and a B.Sc. in Economics from Ferdowsi University of Mashhad.

Job Market Paper

Import Competition, Impaired Driving, and Harm to Others

PDF

Higher unemployment usually comes with fewer traffic deaths, so you might expect job loss to make roads safer. Permanent job loss works differently. When Chinese imports surged in industries like furniture, toys, and electronics in the 1990s and 2000s, many manufacturing towns lost jobs that never came back. Using records of every fatal crash on U.S. roads from 1991 to 2019, I find that more exposed places had about 13 percent more drivers over the legal alcohol limit in fatal crashes than less exposed places in the same state, and about 9 percent more deaths of people in the other vehicles, roughly 430 a year nationwide. Those people never chose the risk, and their deaths are left out when we count what a layoff costs.
