Teaching

Teaching Philosophy

I teach the way I do research: start from a real question and real data, make the core ideas clear, and give students the tools to judge for themselves whether a policy worked.

Strong foundations first. Students do best when the basics are clear, so I begin each course by checking what they already know about the ideas it is built on, and offer short review materials and extra help where needed. I use real policy cases to make abstract concepts concrete; to explain public goods, for example, I work through road tolls and national park entrance fees.

Thinking critically about evidence. In program evaluation, I give students a real policy and ask what we would need to see in the data to say that it worked, and what else could explain the same result. That teaches the method together with its limits and how much the answer depends on its assumptions. Open discussion, where students can challenge the material and each other, builds both their skills and their confidence.

Practice and feedback. Courses with regular problem sets and projects work better than courses that wait for the final exam. Grading about 30 students in Economics of Public Policy showed me that each student gets stuck at a different point, so I give individual comments aimed at the exact step they missed. I also bring hands-on work in Stata, R, and Python into the course and keep the syllabus current with new research, so students leave with skills they can use in policy work.

An inclusive classroom. English is my second language, and teaching in two countries has made me mindful of what students, especially international students, find difficult. I support them with additional materials and one-on-one conversations.
